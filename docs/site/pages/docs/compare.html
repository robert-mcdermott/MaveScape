--- title: Comparisons description: Comparing two sets of scores in a workspace, two replicates, two conditions or two runs (a rescoring, or a MaveDB record's published scores against its counts scored again): the compatibility report that says what the comparison can mean, the agreement statistics with their 95% intervals (Pearson, Spearman, Lin's concordance, Bland–Altman), the Bland–Altman plot, and the files. lede: Do two replicates agree? Does a rescoring reproduce the published scores? Compare puts two sets of scores side by side. Before any number, it says whether the two can be compared, and what their agreement can mean. ---

Two sets of scores, side by side

GRB2's replicates 1 and 2: every item of the report ok, so every statistic is given.

The Compare view (after Map in the view switcher) shows two sets of scores of the open workspace: each a run's condition (its combined scores) or one of its replicates, of one run or two. Pick them under Sides; Swap exchanges them. MaveScape starts with the comparison a workspace most likely asks for: a run scored from a MaveDB record's counts against the record's published scores; otherwise the latest run's first two replicates, or its two conditions, or the last two runs.

The first side is drawn across, the second up, one point per variant scored on both, colored by class. Click a point to see the variant in the inspector, as the first side scored it; the ring follows it. Comparing other score sets (another MaveDB record without opening it, another assay, a predictor) and the comparison drawn across the map come later in 0.4 (roadmap, wave 4).

The compatibility report

The report comes before the statistics. Each item is ok, qualified (the statistics hold, read as the item says), or blocked (no statistics, and why):

  • Target. Variants are matched by name on the same target sequence. Two targets that are different stretches of one UniProt entry, each with its accession and offset (set in Experiment, or by checking the target against UniProt), are matched on the entry's coordinates: their single substitutions, by position in the entry. Two targets with no common reference are not compared, and the report says what to give.
  • Level. Nucleotide variants of one target are matched as written; against a protein-level side, a nucleotide run is read at the protein level, its variants making one protein change merged as on the map.
  • Variants. How many are scored on both sides, and how many on one side only (absent from the other's variants, or there but not scored). The wild type, the reference of both, is left out. Fewer than 10 in common are not compared.
  • Direction. What a higher score means, from each design's readout. When one side says higher means more of the function and the other less, the second is compared negated, so that agreement reads as agreement. A direction not stated is taken as more of the function, and the report says so.
  • Assays. Two different assays (two readouts or methods) can disagree for reasons of biology, not only of measurement.
  • Scale. Two sides are on one scale when they are of one run, or when the same model, normalization, time scale and rescaling made them. Published scores are on their record's own scale, which MaveScape does not know. On different or unknown scales, the correlations are given, and Lin's concordance and Bland–Altman, which measure agreement on one scale, are withheld: two assays on different scales are not read as disagreeing.
  • Independence. Two replicates that share a sample (an input library selected twice) carry its counting error in both, so they agree more than independent measurements would. A replicate against the condition it is combined into agrees in part because one contains the other. One table scored twice, or a record's published scores against its own counts scored again, measures the difference between two analyses, not the experiment's reproducibility.
BRCA1's counts scored against the published scores: correlations only, because the published scores are on the record's own scale, and one experiment analysed twice.

The agreement

Each statistic is given with its 95% interval:

  • Pearson's r, how near the points lie to a straight line, with its interval from Fisher's z.
  • Spearman's ρ, the same for their ranks, with the interval of Bonett and Wright (2000).
  • Lin's concordance correlation ρc (Lin 1989), how near the points lie to the line y = x: Pearson's r (precision) times the accuracy Cb, which falls as the two differ in scale (the scale shift, the ratio of their SDs) or in location (the location shift). Its interval is Lin's (2000) on the z scale.
  • Bland–Altman (1986): the differences (first − second), their mean (the bias) and the limits of agreement, the bias ± 1.96 SDs, between which 95% of the differences lie when they are normal; each with its interval. The trend of the differences with the means says whether the two differ in scale, not only by a bias: a slope beyond 0. The Bland–Altman plot shows each variant's difference against its mean, the bias and the limits.

The statistics equal R's to 13 significant digits (cor.test, DescTools' CCC, BlandAltmanLeh, lm) on seven pairs, among them DHFR's published scores without and with the Lon protease. In simulated pairs of known agreement, from 30 to 1,000 variants, the intervals hold the truth 94–96% of the time. Two are weaker, and the validation reports them as they are: near r = 1, Spearman's interval holds the truth less often (86% with 30 variants, 92% with 200), its ρ biased below the truth there; and with 30 variants on two scales, Lin's holds it 92% of the time.

Read the report with the numbers. Replicates that share their input agree for a reason the experiment does not care about. A rescoring that correlates with the published scores at r 0.76 (BRCA1's saturation genome editing) may differ only in what the authors did to their scores (each exon normalized apart), which the counts do not record.

The files

Matched variants (CSV) writes one row per variant scored on both sides: the variant (on the entry's coordinates when matched there), as each side names it, its class, each side's score and SE as scored, and the difference and mean as compared. Report and statistics (JSON) writes the two sides (each run's id and output hash), how they were matched, the report, the counts and every statistic, with what was withheld and why. FORMATS.md describes both. A comparison is a view: the workspace keeps the runs, each with its record, and the comparison is made again from them.

From a script

Remote control's compare takes the two sides by name ("Run 1: Replicate 1", "Published scores", "Run 1: +Lon"), shows the Compare view, and returns the report and the statistics; export with what: "comparison" writes the matched variants, or, to a path ending in .json, the report and statistics (Remote control).
